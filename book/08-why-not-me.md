# Story 8: Why Not Me?

*Feeling: jealousy and unfairness. Name of Allah: Al-Hakim, The Wise. Example: Imam Ali, peace be upon him, and his simple life as leader.*

---

Maryam got a bicycle.

A purple one, with a basket and a bell and streamers on the handles. She rode it up and down the street for the whole afternoon, ringing the bell, and everyone came out to look.

Noor did not have a bicycle. Noor had asked for one for two birthdays in a row. Baba had said, "Maybe next year, habibti," in the voice that meant no.

Now Noor was in bed, and there was a hard little stone in her chest, and the stone said: *It's not fair. Why does Maryam get everything? Why not me?*

A golden light blinked on.

"That's a stone," said Fanoos. "I can tell by the shape of your face."

"It's not fair," said Noor.

"Come on," said Fanoos. "There's a peacock I want you to meet."

---

The peacock was standing in a clearing in the Lantern Garden with its tail spread out, and its tail was the most beautiful thing Noor had ever seen. Green and blue and gold, with eyes in it that seemed to look back.

And the peacock was crying.

"What's wrong?" said Noor. "You're beautiful."

"Look at the nightingale," said the peacock, pointing its beak at a small brown bird on a branch. The nightingale was singing, and the song was so lovely the lanterns swayed.

"I can't sing," said the peacock. "I open my mouth and it comes out like a broken door. The nightingale gets the voice. Why not me?"

Noor looked at the nightingale. The nightingale was looking at the peacock's tail, and it had stopped singing, and its small brown feathers looked very plain, and it looked exactly as sad as the peacock.

"Oh," said Noor.

"What?" said the peacock.

"The nightingale is looking at your tail," said Noor, "the way you're looking at its voice."

The peacock turned around. The nightingale looked away quickly.

Noor sat down on the grass between them. "I have a stone in my chest," she said, "because my friend got a bicycle. And I've had it all evening and it's made me forget about everything I do have. My brother made me a paper bird. I have a grandmother who tells me stories. I have a prayer mat that smells like home. But I couldn't see any of it because of the bicycle."

"That's how stones work," said Fanoos. "They're very small, and they block the whole view."

"Do you know what my grandmother says?" Noor went on. "She says Allah is Al-Hakim, The Wise. It means that everything He gives, and everything He doesn't give, has a reason, even if we can't see it yet. Allah gave the nightingale a voice and the peacock a tail, and He didn't get it wrong. He knew what each one needed."

"But what if I *need* a voice?" said the peacock.

"Then Allah would have given you one," said Noor. "He's not stingy. He's wise. And there's another thing. Imam Ali, peace be upon him. He was the leader of all the Muslims, the most important person in the whole land. He could have had a palace and a hundred bicycles. And he lived in a plain house and wore patched clothes and ate simple bread, on purpose, so that the poorest person wouldn't feel far from him. He had everything and he chose to want little. That's the opposite of a stone."

The peacock folded its tail slowly, and looked at it for the first time as if it were its own.

"It is quite a good tail," it said. "Alhamdulillah."

"It is a very good song," said the nightingale shyly. "Alhamdulillah."

"Would you like to hear it?" said the nightingale.

"Would you like to see it?" said the peacock.

And Noor sat between them while one sang and the other shone, and the stone in her chest, though she wasn't watching it, got smaller and smaller until she couldn't find it any more.

---

In the morning Noor went next door.

"Can I have a go on your bicycle?" she asked Maryam.

"Yes," said Maryam, who had been hoping someone would ask. "Can I see your paper bird? Adam told me he made you one."

That afternoon, Noor rode the purple bicycle up and down the street with a badly folded paper bird sitting in the basket, and she rang the bell, and she said *Alhamdulillah*, and she meant it.

## Questions for you

1. When did you last feel a stone in your chest because someone had something you didn't?
2. The peacock wanted a voice. The nightingale wanted a tail. What did each one already have?
3. Can you think of one thing you have that someone else might wish they had?
4. Imam Ali could have had a palace. Why do you think he chose a plain house?
5. What are three things you can say *Alhamdulillah* for right now, tonight?

## Something to try

**Three before sleep.** Every night, before you sleep, say three things you are thankful for. They have to be different from yesterday's. Small ones count: warm socks, a good dinner, a joke. By the end of a week you will have twenty-one, and the stone will have nowhere to sit.

## A dua to remember

**لَئِن شَكَرْتُمْ لَأَزِيدَنَّكُمْ**

*La'in shakartum la'azidannakum.*

"If you are grateful, I will surely give you more." (Quran 14:7)

## For grown-ups

Jealousy in children is normal and usually loud. "It's not fair" is one of the first moral sentences a child learns. Do not shame it, and do not fix it by buying the thing. Name it, sit with it, and redirect attention to what the child has. Gratitude practice, done nightly and specifically, is one of the best-supported habits for children's wellbeing, and it fits naturally with saying *Alhamdulillah*. The simplicity of Imam Ali's life as caliph, his plain clothes and food, is widely related in Shia sources including Nahj al-Balagha *(verify the specific accounts you tell with your scholar)*. If a child's jealousy turns into constant comparison, self-hatred, or cruelty to a sibling, look at what is underneath: often it is a worry about being loved less.
