# Story 7: The Bad Dream

*Feeling: nightmares. Name of Allah: As-Salam, The Source of Peace. Example: the tasbih the Prophet, peace be upon him and his family, taught Sayyida Fatima, peace be upon her.*

---

Noor woke up with a shout.

She didn't know what she'd shouted. She didn't know where she was. The dream was still on her, like a wet coat. Something had been chasing her, something with no face, and the door wouldn't open, and she'd been calling and nobody came.

Now she was in her bed and the room was dark and her heart was banging and she did not want to close her eyes ever again, because the dream was in there, behind her eyelids, waiting.

A golden light blinked on.

"That was a bad one," said Fanoos.

"I'm not going back to sleep," said Noor. "I'm going to stay awake all night."

"That's a long time," said Fanoos gently. "And you'd be very tired tomorrow. But we don't have to sleep yet. Come to the garden. It's always safe there."

---

The garden was very dark tonight. Only a few lanterns were lit. And in the middle of the darkest part, Noor could hear something crying.

It was an owl. A small owl, on a low branch, with its feathers puffed out and its huge eyes wide open.

"I won't close them," the owl said, before Noor had even asked. "If I close my eyes the dream comes back. So I'm keeping them open. Forever."

"I had a dream too," said Noor. "Something with no face. A door that wouldn't open."

"Mine had teeth," said the owl. "Where do they come from, the bad dreams? Are they real?"

Noor thought about this. "My grandmother says some dreams are just the mind sorting out the day, like emptying a bag. And some bad ones are from Shaytan, because he likes to frighten us, especially at night, when we're alone. But she also says a bad dream from Shaytan can't hurt you. It can only scare you. Scaring isn't hurting."

"It felt like hurting," said the owl.

"I know," said Noor. "So she taught me what to do."

The owl blinked, its first blink in a long time. "What?"

"When you wake up from a bad dream," said Noor, counting on her fingers, "first you say *A'udhu billahi min ash-shaytan ir-rajim*, I seek protection with Allah from Shaytan. Then you turn over and lie on your other side, so your body knows it's a new start. And you don't tell the dream to anyone in a way that makes it bigger. You just say, it was a bad dream, and Allah is As-Salam."

"What does As-Salam mean?"

"It's one of Allah's names. It means The Source of Peace. All the peace in the whole world comes from Him. So if you want peace, you ask the one who has all of it."

"And then?" said the owl. "How do I close my eyes?"

Fanoos glowed. "Noor, tell him about the tasbih."

"Oh!" said Noor. "This is my favourite. One day Sayyida Fatima, peace be upon her, was very tired. Her hands were sore from all the work, and she asked her father the Prophet for help. And instead of a helper, he gave her something better. He taught her to say, before she slept, *Allahu Akbar* thirty-four times, *Alhamdulillah* thirty-three times, and *Subhanallah* thirty-three times. And she did it every night for the rest of her life. And so did Imam Ali. And so do we."

"That's a hundred things to say," said the owl.

"That's the point," said Noor. "By the time you get to the end, your mouth is tired and your heart is slow and there's no room left in your head for teeth."

The owl closed its eyes. "*Allahu Akbar*," it said. "*Allahu Akbar. Allahu Akbar...*"

Noor counted on her fingers for it. By twenty-one the owl's feathers had gone flat. By *Alhamdulillah* number twelve its head had drooped. Somewhere in the middle of *Subhanallah* it was asleep, and it did not dream of teeth.

---

In her bed, Noor said, "*A'udhu billahi min ash-shaytan ir-rajim.*" She turned over onto her other side. She said, in a small firm voice, "It was a bad dream. Allah is As-Salam."

Then she started. *Allahu Akbar. Allahu Akbar. Allahu Akbar.*

She got to twenty-eight.

## Questions for you

1. What do you do when you wake up from a bad dream? Who do you go to?
2. Noor said scaring isn't the same as hurting. What's the difference?
3. Why do you think the Prophet gave Sayyida Fatima the tasbih instead of a helper?
4. What is the hardest part of going back to sleep after a bad dream?
5. Who is awake and keeping the peace while you sleep?

## Something to try

**The tasbih of Sayyida Fatima.** Tonight, before you sleep, say *Allahu Akbar* 34 times, *Alhamdulillah* 33 times, and *Subhanallah* 33 times. Use your fingers or a tasbih. If you fall asleep in the middle, that is fine. That is the idea.

## A dua to remember

**بِاسْمِكَ اللَّهُمَّ أَمُوتُ وَأَحْيَا**

*Bismika Allahumma amutu wa ahya.*

"In Your name, O Allah, I die and I live." *(A well-known dua before sleep; verify the wording in Shia sources with your scholar.)*

## For grown-ups

Nightmares peak between ages three and ten and rise sharply after frightening events. A child who refuses to sleep after a nightmare is not being difficult; the fear is real and physical. Help by staying calm, keeping the light low, and giving the child something to do rather than something to think about. The practices in this story, seeking refuge from Shaytan and turning onto the other side, are widely taught for bad dreams *(verify the sourcing in Shia hadith collections with your scholar)*. The tasbih of Sayyida Fatima is among the best-attested practices in the Shia tradition, reported in al-Kafi and elsewhere, and is recommended before sleep *(verify)*. Its rhythmic repetition is also, in plain terms, an excellent calming technique. If nightmares happen most nights for more than a few weeks, or the child relives a real event in them, see a doctor or counsellor.
