# Bookkeeping and VAT for Small Business Owners
## A plain-language guide, including working in two currencies

Working title. Target length: 18,000 to 22,000 words. Ten chapters of roughly 1,500 to 2,200 words each, plus a short introduction and an appendix.

### Positioning decision (read this first)

The accounting software this repo contains is built for Lebanon: Lebanese pound as main currency, a secondary currency, VAT tracked as payable versus refundable. That is real expertise, and it decides two things about the book.

1. **The core of the book is jurisdiction-neutral.** Double-entry, the chart of accounts, how VAT works mechanically, month-end close and reading reports are the same everywhere. A book limited to one small country has too few Kindle buyers.
2. **Two-currency bookkeeping is the distinctive angle.** Almost no beginner bookkeeping book covers running a business where prices, bank accounts and invoices sit in two currencies at once. That is daily reality in Lebanon, Egypt, Turkey, Argentina, Nigeria and many other markets, and for freelancers anywhere who get paid in a foreign currency. This is the chapter that makes the book different from the hundreds already on Amazon.

Every place a rule depends on local law (VAT rate, filing frequency, registration threshold, record retention) gets a short marked box: **"Check your local rule"** with the question the reader must answer for their own country. Where useful, give Lebanon as the worked example, clearly labelled as one example.

### How we write this without an AI-generated disclosure

Amazon KDP asks whether the text is AI-generated. Text drafted by Claude counts. Text you write that Claude restructures, tightens and proofreads is AI-assisted and does not need disclosure. So the working method is:

## Introduction: who this book is for (600 to 900 words)

Who: owners of small businesses and freelancers who do their own books or want to understand what their accountant is doing. Not for accounting students. No prior knowledge assumed.

Promise: by the end the reader can set up a simple set of books, record a month of activity, calculate the VAT they owe, and read their own profit and loss and balance sheet.

**What you supply:** one or two real stories of a business owner who got into trouble because their records were bad (anonymised), and your own one-paragraph background.

## Chapter 1: Why your records matter more than your revenue

- The three jobs of bookkeeping: know if you are making money, know what you owe, prove it to the tax authority.
- Why a spreadsheet of bank transactions is not a set of books.
- The cost of finding out in December what you should have known in March.
- The minimum a small business must keep, and for how long. *Check your local rule box.*

**What you supply:** the mistakes you see most often from clients or users of your software.

## Chapter 2: The five kinds of account

- Assets, liabilities, equity, income, expenses, each in one sentence and one everyday example.
- The accounting equation in words, not symbols.
- What a chart of accounts is and why a small business needs a short one, not a long one.
- A sample chart of accounts for a service business and one for a trading business (appendix has the full lists).

**What you supply:** the chart of accounts you recommend by default, and the accounts people wrongly add or forget.

## Chapter 3: Double-entry without the jargon

- Every transaction touches at least two accounts. Why this catches errors.
- Debits and credits explained by account type, with a one-page reference table.
- Journal, ledger, trial balance: what each is for.
- Five worked transactions, each shown as a two-line entry.

**What you supply:** the analogy you personally use to explain debit and credit to non-accountants.

## Chapter 4: Setting up your books

- Choosing a start date and entering opening balances.
- Bank accounts, cash box, and why cash needs its own discipline.
- Cost centres and projects: when a small business benefits from tagging transactions this way, and when it is overkill.
- Choosing a tool: paper, spreadsheet, or software. Honest criteria. Mention your own software once, briefly, as one option among several.

**What you supply:** your cost centre and project examples, and your criteria for when software is worth paying for.

## Chapter 5: Recording a month of activity

- The main transaction types: sales invoice, purchase invoice, receipt, payment, expense, transfer, general journal.
- Customers and suppliers as sub-ledgers: who owes you, whom you owe.
- Handling partial payments, discounts, returns, and bad debts.
- A worked example: one full month for a small shop, entry by entry.

**What you supply:** the worked-month example. Pick a business type you know well and give the real-looking figures.

## Chapter 6: VAT, explained properly

- What VAT is and who actually pays it. Output VAT versus input VAT.
- VAT payable, VAT refundable, and the netting at period end.
- VAT on purchases of assets versus purchases for resale versus expenses, and why the accounts are kept separately.
- Registration thresholds, rates, filing periods, exempt versus zero-rated. *Check your local rule box for each.*
- The six VAT mistakes that cost small businesses money.
- Worked example: a quarter of sales and purchases, ending with the return figure.

**What you supply:** the Lebanese rules as the worked example (rate, filing period, threshold, with the date you last verified them), and the real errors you have seen in VAT returns.

## Chapter 7: Working in two currencies

- Main currency, secondary currency, and other currencies: what each means for the books.
- Which rate to use and when: transaction date, payment date, period end.
- Realised versus unrealised exchange gains and losses, in plain words.
- Revaluing foreign currency balances at month end.
- Keeping customer and supplier balances in the currency of the invoice.
- Pricing and invoicing when the currency swings: practical policies, not theory.
- Worked example: an invoice issued in one currency, paid in another, at a different rate.

**What you supply:** this whole chapter leans on your real experience. Write it first, in whatever language is fastest for you.

## Chapter 8: Closing the month

- Why a monthly close exists and what "closing" locks.
- The checklist: bank reconciliation, cash count, unpaid invoices review, VAT accounts, currency revaluation, accruals and prepayments.
- Fixing an error after close versus before close.
- Year-end: what changes, what your accountant will ask for.

**What you supply:** your month-end checklist, as you actually use it.

## Chapter 9: Reading your own reports

- Trial balance: what it proves and what it does not.
- Profit and loss: reading it line by line, gross margin, the three questions to ask.
- Balance sheet: what you own, what you owe, what is left.
- Account statement for a customer or supplier.
- Cash flow versus profit: why a profitable business runs out of cash.
- Reports by cost centre or project: when they change a decision.

**What you supply:** one anonymised set of reports from a small business, and what you would say to the owner about them.

## Chapter 10: Working with your accountant and the tax authority

- Dividing the work: what the owner does, what the accountant does.
- What to hand over each month and each year, as a list.
- Being audit-ready: documents, numbering, retention. *Check your local rule box.*
- Red flags that attract attention.
- When to move from doing it yourself to hiring help.

**What you supply:** the handover list you would want from a client, and the audit stories.
