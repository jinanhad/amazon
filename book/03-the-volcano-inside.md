# Story 3: The Volcano Inside

*Feeling: anger. Name of Allah: Al-Halim, The Forbearing. Example: Imam Ali, peace be upon him, and his forbearance.*

---

Adam broke it.

It was the paper bird Noor had spent all afternoon folding, the one with the tiny wings, and her little brother had sat on it, and now it was flat.

Something hot went up from Noor's tummy to her face. She shouted. She shouted a word she wasn't allowed to say. She pushed Adam, and Adam fell over and cried, and Mama came running and said, "Noor! Go to your room!"

So now she was in her room, and she was still hot, and she was angry at Adam and angry at Mama and angry at herself, and the angriest thing was that she didn't know how to stop.

A golden light blinked on.

"Go away," said Noor.

"All right," said Fanoos, and started to fade.

"No, wait." Noor put her face in her hands. "I'm not angry at you. I'm just angry."

"I know," said Fanoos. "It feels like a volcano, doesn't it? Come and meet someone."

---

In the middle of the Lantern Garden, where Noor had never been before, there was a mountain. And the mountain was rumbling.

"Who's there?" Noor called.

"GO AWAY," said the mountain. Smoke came out of the top.

"That's what I said too," said Noor. "A minute ago."

The mountain was quiet for a second. Then it said, in a smaller voice, "Everyone always goes away when I get hot. Then I'm hot *and* alone."

Noor sat down at the bottom of the mountain. The ground was warm.

"What made you hot?"

"A bird built a nest on my head. I told it not to. It did it anyway. So I shook, and the nest fell, and now the bird is crying, and everyone says I'm bad."

"You're not bad," said Noor. "You were angry. Those are different things."

"Are they?"

Noor thought about Adam and the flat paper bird. "I think angry is a feeling. It comes when something feels unfair. It isn't wrong to feel it. But when I'm angry I do things I don't mean. I pushed my brother. That part was wrong. The feeling wasn't."

The mountain rumbled thoughtfully.

"So what do I do with the hot?"

Fanoos landed on a rock. "Noor, do you know what our Imams taught about anger?"

Noor did know this one. Her teacher had said it.

"If you're standing, sit down," she said. "If you're still angry, lie down. And say *A'udhu billahi min ash-shaytan ir-rajim*, which means, I ask Allah to protect me from Shaytan, because Shaytan loves it when we're angry, and he tries to make the volcano bigger."

"I'm a mountain," said the mountain. "I'm already sitting down."

"Then lie down," said Noor.

Slowly, with a lot of grumbling, the mountain leaned over until it was lying on its side like a sleeping cat. The smoke from its top drifted sideways and thinned.

"That's a bit better," it admitted.

"Now drink some water," said Noor, because Mama always said that, and she cupped some from the stream and poured it on the mountain's warm side. It hissed and steamed and cooled.

"And here's the last thing," said Fanoos. "Do you know what Al-Halim means?"

"No."

"It's one of Allah's names. It means The Forbearing. It means that Allah sees every wrong thing people do, every single day, and He is never hasty. He doesn't rush to punish. He waits, and He gives chances, and He is patient, even when He has every right not to be."

"And Imam Ali was like that," said Noor. "Peace be upon him. My grandmother says he was the bravest man who ever lived, stronger than anyone. But when people were rude to him, he didn't get hot. He was gentle. He said a person who can hold back his anger is stronger than a person who can win a fight. If the strongest man in the world could be halim, maybe we can be a little bit halim with one bird."

The mountain looked at the crying bird, and the fallen nest.

"I could hold still," it said. "While it builds a new one."

---

In her room, Noor lay down on the floor. It was cool. She said, "*A'udhu billahi min ash-shaytan ir-rajim.*" She drank the water from the cup on her desk.

The hot went down from her face to her tummy and then it just went.

She went to find Adam. He was still sniffing.

"I'm sorry I pushed you," she said. "I was angry about the bird. I'm still a bit sad about it. But I shouldn't have pushed."

Adam looked at her. Then he held out something. It was a piece of paper, folded very badly, with something that might be wings.

"I made you a new one," he said.

It was terrible. It was the best bird Noor had ever seen.

---

## Questions for you

1. Where does the hot go in your body when you're angry? Your face? Your hands? Your tummy?
2. Noor said angry is a feeling, and pushing is an action. What's the difference?
3. What made the mountain angry? Was that a fair reason to feel angry? Was shaking the nest off a fair thing to do?
4. Imam Ali was the strongest, and also the most gentle. Which is harder?
5. What is one thing you could do next time before the volcano erupts?

## Something to try

**Sit, drink, say.** Next time you feel the hot rising: sit down if you're standing, lie down if sitting doesn't help. Drink a cup of water slowly. Say "*A'udhu billahi min ash-shaytan ir-rajim*." Then decide what to do. The volcano can't erupt while you're lying down drinking water.

## A dua to remember

**أَعُوذُ بِاللَّهِ مِنَ الشَّيْطَانِ الرَّجِيمِ**

*A'udhu billahi min ash-shaytan ir-rajim.*

"I seek protection with Allah from Shaytan, the rejected one."

## For grown-ups

Anger in children is usually a second feeling. Underneath is hurt, unfairness, fear or tiredness. Punishing the anger itself teaches a child to hide it, not to manage it. Separate the two clearly every time: "It's all right to be angry. It's not all right to hit." The advice to sit down when angry while standing, and to lie down if still angry, is reported from Imam al-Baqir, peace be upon him, in al-Kafi, in the chapter on anger *(verify the narration and its wording with your scholar)*. Sayings of Imam Ali on anger and self-restraint appear in Nahj al-Balagha and in Ghurar al-Hikam *(verify)*. Model it yourself out loud when you get angry; it is the most powerful lesson in this book. If a child's anger is frequent, explosive, or hurts others regularly, a counsellor can help find what is underneath it.
