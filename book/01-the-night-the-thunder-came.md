# Story 1: The Night the Thunder Came

*Feeling: fear of loud noises. Name of Allah: Al-Hafiz, The Protector. Example: Imam Ali, peace be upon him, on the night he slept in the Prophet's bed.*

---

The first boom shook the window.

Noor pulled the blanket over her head. Her heart was going *thud, thud, thud*, faster than the rain. The second boom was louder. She pressed her hands over her ears and squeezed her eyes shut, but the noise got inside anyway.

"Mama!" she called. But Mama was with the baby, and the baby was crying too.

Then, in the dark under the blanket, a small light blinked on.

It was a firefly. A tiny, warm, golden firefly, sitting on her knee.

"Hello, Noor," said the firefly. "I'm Fanoos. I come when a feeling is too big to hold by yourself."

"I'm not scared," said Noor, though her voice was shaking.

"Your heart is going very fast," said Fanoos kindly. "That's what a body does when it wants to keep you safe. It isn't bad. It's just loud. Would you like to come somewhere quieter for a minute?"

Noor nodded.

Fanoos glowed brighter, and brighter, until the blanket wasn't a blanket any more. It was a gate. And behind the gate was a garden, lit by hundreds of little lanterns hanging from the trees.

"The Lantern Garden," said Fanoos. "Every creature here has a feeling. Tonight, someone has the same one as you."

---

Under a fig tree, Noor found a rabbit. It was curled into a ball so tight it looked like a stone. Its ears were flat against its back, and it was trembling.

"What's wrong?" Noor whispered.

"The sky is breaking," said the rabbit. "Can't you hear it? Something is coming to get me."

Noor listened. Far away, even here, there was thunder. But here it sounded softer, like someone rolling a barrel down a hill.

"It's thunder," said Noor. "It's very loud, but it's far away. It's not coming to get you."

"How do you know?" said the rabbit.

Noor thought. She didn't know, not really. Nobody can promise the sky. But then she remembered something her grandmother told her.

"Do you know about Imam Ali?" she asked the rabbit. "Peace be upon him. One night, bad men were coming to hurt the Prophet, peace be upon him and his family. The Prophet had to leave the city in secret. And Imam Ali lay down in the Prophet's bed, under the Prophet's blanket, so the bad men would think the Prophet was still there. He knew they were coming. He heard them outside. And he lay there all night, and he was not afraid."

"Why not?" said the rabbit. "I would be afraid."

"Because he knew Allah is Al-Hafiz," said Noor. "It means The Protector. Allah is awake all night. He never sleeps and He never looks away. Imam Ali trusted that whatever happened, Allah was looking after him. And Allah did."

The rabbit's ears lifted, just a little.

"Does Allah look after rabbits?"

"He looks after everything," said Noor. "Every bird. Every ant. Every rabbit. Every girl."

"But I'm still shaking," said the rabbit.

"That's all right," said Fanoos, landing on the rabbit's nose. "Shaking isn't wrong. Let's give your body something to do. Noor, show him the three breaths."

Fanoos glowed once, twice, three times, slowly, and Noor understood.

"Breathe in while the light gets bright," she told the rabbit. "Breathe out while it gets soft."

In. Out. The rabbit's ball got a little looser.

In. Out. Its ears stood up.

In. Out. It uncurled and sat like a rabbit again.

"Now say this with me," said Noor, and she said the words her grandmother said: "*Hasbunallahu wa ni'mal-wakeel.* Allah is enough for us, and He is the best one to take care of things."

The rabbit said it too, in a small rabbit voice.

The thunder rolled again, far away. The rabbit flinched. But it didn't curl up.

"It's still loud," it said.

"Yes," said Noor. "It's still loud. And we're still all right."

---

When Noor opened her eyes, she was under her blanket again. The rain was still falling. Somewhere the thunder was still grumbling.

Her heart was going *thud... thud... thud*, slow now, like footsteps.

She breathed in. She breathed out. She thought of Imam Ali under the Prophet's blanket, listening to the noise outside, not afraid. She whispered, "*Hasbunallahu wa ni'mal-wakeel.*"

Mama came in with the baby, who had stopped crying.

"Are you all right, habibti?"

"It was loud," said Noor. "But Allah is Al-Hafiz. And I did the three breaths, like Imam Ali would."

Mama smiled and sat on the bed and put her hand on Noor's chest, and Noor felt her own heart, slow and steady, and she was asleep before the next thunder came.

---

## Questions for you

1. What noises make your heart go fast?
2. Where in your body do you feel it when you are scared? Your tummy, your chest, your hands?
3. Imam Ali heard the bad men outside and still lay in the bed. What do you think he was thinking about?
4. Noor said "It's still loud, and we're still all right." Can both things be true at the same time?
5. Who looks after you when you are asleep and cannot look after yourself?

## Something to try

**The three breaths.** Put one hand on your tummy. Breathe in slowly while you count to three in your head. Breathe out slowly while you count to three. Do it three times. Feel your hand go up and down. Your body cannot race and breathe slowly at the same time.

## A dua to remember

**حَسْبُنَا اللَّهُ وَنِعْمَ الْوَكِيلُ**

*Hasbunallahu wa ni'mal-wakeel.*

"Allah is enough for us, and He is the best one to take care of things." (Quran 3:173)

## For grown-ups

Fear of loud noises is one of the most common childhood fears, and it is much stronger in children who have heard frightening sounds for real. A racing heart, shaking, covering ears and wanting to hide are the body's alarm system, not misbehaviour. Two things help most: a calm adult nearby, and something simple for the body to do. Slow breathing works because it is physically incompatible with panic. Do not promise that nothing bad will ever happen; children know adults cannot promise that. Say instead what is true: "I am here. You are safe right now. Allah is watching over us." The story of Laylat al-Mabit, the night Imam Ali slept in the Prophet's bed before the hijra, is widely taught in Shia tradition and linked by commentators to Quran 2:207 *(verify with your scholar)*. If the fear does not fade over weeks, or the child startles violently, replays the event, or cannot sleep, please see a doctor or counsellor.
