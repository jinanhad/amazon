# Story 4: The Worry Cloud

*Feeling: worry about tomorrow. Name of Allah: Al-Wakeel, The Trustee. Example: Imam al-Mahdi, may Allah hasten his reappearance, who is near and cares for us.*

---

Tomorrow was the spelling test.

Noor had practised. She had practised at the table, and in the bath, and walking to the shop. But now it was night and the words had all fallen out of her head, and instead a cloud had come in.

The cloud said: *What if you forget everything? What if you get the lowest mark? What if the teacher reads it out? What if everyone laughs?*

Noor turned over. The cloud turned over with her.

*What if, what if, what if.*

A golden light blinked on.

"Your head is very busy," said Fanoos.

"I can't stop it," said Noor. "I keep thinking about tomorrow and tomorrow hasn't even happened yet."

"That's what a worry is," said Fanoos. "It's a story about tomorrow, told by a cloud. Shall we go and see one?"

---

In the Lantern Garden, up in the branches of the tallest tree, sat a squirrel. It was surrounded by nuts. Hundreds of nuts. Thousands of nuts, piled up in every fork of every branch, and it was still running back and forth, carrying more.

"Hello," said Noor. "You've got a lot of nuts."

"Not enough!" said the squirrel, not stopping. "Winter is coming! What if it's a long winter? What if it's the longest winter ever? What if the nuts go bad? What if another squirrel steals them? What if I forget where I put them?"

"Have you ever run out of nuts in the winter?" asked Noor.

The squirrel stopped for the first time. "No."

"Not once?"

"Not once. But what if *this* time..."

Noor climbed up and sat on a branch. "I have a test tomorrow," she said. "And I keep saying what if too. What if I forget. What if I fail. My grandmother says a what-if is like a nut you can never finish eating. You bite it and there's another one inside."

"So what do I do?" said the squirrel. "Stop gathering nuts? Then I really would run out."

"No," said Fanoos. "Gathering nuts is your part. You do your part properly. But then there's a second part, and that part isn't yours."

"Whose is it?"

"Allah's," said Noor. "He is Al-Wakeel. It means The Trustee, the one you hand things to and He takes care of them. The Quran says that whoever trusts in Allah, He is enough for him. You gather the nuts. You can't make the winter short. So you hand the winter to Allah."

The squirrel looked at its thousands of nuts.

"I have done my part," it said slowly. "Haven't I?"

"You did your part a long time ago," said Noor. "Now you're just carrying the cloud around."

"And here is something else," said Fanoos. "Noor, who do you make dua for every day, the one who is waiting?"

"Imam al-Mahdi," said Noor. "May Allah hasten his reappearance. My grandmother says he is alive, and hidden, and he knows about us, and he cares what happens to us, even the small things. Even spelling tests. Even nuts."

"So you are not alone with the cloud," said Fanoos. "Allah is Al-Wakeel, and the Imam is making dua for the people, and you have done your part. That is three things. The cloud is only one thing."

The squirrel sat down among its nuts. For the first time it looked at them instead of counting them.

"That's quite a lot of nuts," it said. "Alhamdulillah."

---

Back in bed, Noor said to the cloud: "I practised. That was my part. The rest isn't mine."

She said the dua her grandmother taught her for when a worry is heavy, the one that starts *Ilahi 'azuma al-bala'*, O my Allah, the trouble has grown great. Then she said, "*Hasbiyallahu la ilaha illa huwa, 'alayhi tawakkaltu.* Allah is enough for me. There is no god but Him. I put my trust in Him."

The cloud didn't vanish. But it got smaller, and it moved to the corner of the room, and Noor fell asleep.

In the morning she spelled seven words right out of ten. The teacher did not read it out. Nobody laughed. And Noor thought, *I carried that cloud all night for seven out of ten.*

---

## Questions for you

1. What is a what-if that visits you at night?
2. The squirrel had thousands of nuts and was still afraid. Why didn't the nuts make the fear go away?
3. What is your part in the thing you're worried about? What part isn't yours?
4. Has a worry ever come true exactly the way you imagined it?
5. Who is looking after tomorrow while you are asleep?

## Something to try

**Two lists.** Take a piece of paper. On one side write "My part" and list what you can actually do. On the other side write "Allah's part" and list everything you can't control. Do your part. Then fold the paper in half so only "Allah's part" shows, and say, "*Hasbiyallah*, Allah is enough for me."

## A dua to remember

**حَسْبِيَ اللَّهُ لَا إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا هُوَ عَلَيْهِ تَوَكَّلْتُ**

*Hasbiyallahu la ilaha illa huwa, 'alayhi tawakkaltu.*

"Allah is enough for me. There is no god but Him. In Him I put my trust." (Quran 9:129)

And for a heavy worry, Dua al-Faraj, which begins *Ilahi 'azuma al-bala'*, "O my Allah, the trouble has grown great." *(Wording to be given in full from Mafatih al-Jinan after verification.)*

## For grown-ups

Worry in children shows up as "what if" questions, stomach aches, trouble falling asleep, and asking for reassurance again and again. Reassurance alone does not work for long, because the worry simply asks again. What works is teaching the child to sort what they control from what they do not, do the first, and hand over the second. That is tawakkul in a form a child can hold. The Quran verse "whoever puts his trust in Allah, He is enough for him" is 65:3. Dua al-Faraj is a short, well-loved supplication in the Shia tradition and appears in Mafatih al-Jinan *(verify the full text and attribution with your scholar)*. Teaching the child about Imam al-Mahdi as someone alive who cares for them is a source of comfort in Shia upbringing; keep it warm and simple at this age. If worry stops a child from going to school, sleeping, or eating for weeks, seek help.
